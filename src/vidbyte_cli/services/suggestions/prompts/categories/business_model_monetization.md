# Business Model and Monetization

## Description

A business-model-and-monetization suggestion changes how an offering creates value and captures part
of that value as revenue. It covers subscriptions, services, licensing, marketplaces, bundles,
usage-based charges, and other pricing or packaging structures. The category connects what customers
receive with what they pay, when they pay, and how reliably they continue paying. It also accounts for
the cost and operational burden of delivering the promised value. A sound idea states whose incentives
change and which economic assumption the change is meant to test. It should use a reversible pricing
or packaging experiment when the evidence is too weak for a permanent model change.

## Why use / use cases

- **Customers receive recurring value but pay once.** A one-time transaction may not match ongoing
  service or usage. Use this category when renewal value and delivery cost can be measured.
- **The price does not reflect perceived value.** Customers may pay for outcomes while the business
  charges for an unrelated unit. Use it when a pricing metric could better align incentives.
- **A free or low-priced tier creates adoption without sustainability.** Access may grow while support
  and infrastructure costs accumulate. Use this lens to test a credible conversion or expansion path.
- **Different customers have different willingness to pay.** One package may overcharge small users
  and undercharge high-value users. Use it when segmentation can be made understandable and fair.
- **Services and product capabilities can be bundled.** Customers may value a complete outcome more
  than separate components. Use the category when the bundle's margin and delivery responsibilities are clear.
- **A marketplace or licensing route could change value capture.** The business may create value by
  enabling exchanges or granting rights instead of delivering every unit itself. Use it when roles and
  incentives can be specified.
- **Revenue growth may hide unhealthy economics.** More sales can worsen support, fulfillment, or
  acquisition costs. Use this lens when contribution margin or payback must be tested.
- **A pricing change needs a safe trial.** A permanent change can damage trust or retention before its
  value is known. Use it when a cohort, pilot, or grandfathering plan can produce evidence.

## Things to consider

- What customer value is being charged for, and how often is it received?
- Who pays, who uses, and whose budget or approval controls the purchase?
- Which pricing unit makes value and cost easiest to understand?
- What delivery, support, acquisition, and infrastructure costs scale with usage?
- How will the model affect retention, expansion, refunds, or trust?
- Which segments need different packaging without creating confusing exceptions?
- What economic metric would falsify the proposal: margin, payback, conversion, or retention?
- What bounded cohort or pilot can test the change without trapping existing customers?

## When not to use

Do not use this category when the main uncertainty is who wants the offer, how people hear about it,
or why they should remember it. Avoid pricing changes that have no stated value hypothesis or cost
model. Use business and growth for a plan spanning several mechanisms and customer relationship and
service when the central improvement happens after purchase.
