# Feedback

## Description
A feedback suggestion seeks a specific reaction from someone positioned to notice what the caller cannot. It names three things: what to show, who should see it, and which question will produce a response worth acting on. The artifact has to be developed enough to reveal the behaviour or judgment in question, and honest enough about its own state that the reaction is about the work rather than about its polish. Timing matters as much as content, because a response that arrives after the decision is irreversible costs the reviewer's time and returns nothing. The category exists to import a perspective, not to distribute responsibility. A caller who already has the evidence needed to decide is not seeking feedback but company.

The suggestion must distinguish decision-relevant signal from approval, since most unstructured requests for comment return politeness. The question should be shaped so that its answer is evidence: what the reader understood, where they hesitated, which interpretation occurred to them first, what they would do next. It should be clear in advance which responses would change the work and which would not, because a request that cannot be acted on is a request that should not be made. The suggestion should say how conflicting or biased responses will be interpreted, since averaging disagreement into consensus destroys the information that made the disagreement useful. It should name the revision window the response feeds, so the reviewer knows their effort has somewhere to go. It should also match the reviewer to the question, because expertise in one dimension does not transfer to another.

## Why use
Use this category when the limiting factor is a perspective the caller structurally cannot hold. Familiarity with one's own work removes exactly the information that matters most about how it lands: what is confusing, what is assumed, what a newcomer does first. No amount of care substitutes for this, because the blind spot is created by the knowledge that makes the work possible. Someone else's reaction is the only instrument that reads it.

The category also front-loads a cost that otherwise compounds. Discovering a misunderstanding at the point of use is far more expensive than discovering it in a ten-minute conversation, and the gap grows with everything built on top of the misunderstanding in between. Recommending feedback early is usually a recommendation to pay a small, certain cost instead of a large, probable one. That argument is strongest when the artifact is mature enough to provoke a real reaction and early enough that reacting to it can still change something.

Distinguish it from the categories it sits beside. Verification checks whether a claim is true, which is a question about the world rather than about someone's experience of the work. Investigation gathers facts that already exist somewhere, and if the missing information is factual rather than experiential, asking a person is the slower path. Experiment measures behaviour under a controlled intervention and is the right choice when what people say and what they do are likely to diverge. Goal clarification settles what the work is for, which must happen before a reaction to it can mean anything. Use feedback when the needed input is a judgment or an experience that only another person can supply.

## Use cases
- **A user may hit friction the caller cannot see.** Familiarity hides confusion, hesitation, and unmet need. Watch a representative interaction rather than inferring it.
- **An expert can spot a consequential flaw early.** A reviewer with standing may see technical, legal, operational, or domain risk. Ask while the artifact is ready for that judgment and revision is still affordable.
- **Several interpretations of the work are plausible.** A reaction shows whether the intended meaning or value lands. Ask which interpretation occurred, not whether the reader liked it.
- **A stakeholder must accept a trade.** The caller may need to learn which cost, scope, or risk is tolerable to an affected party. Structure the conversation around a decision rather than ceremonial approval.
- **A prototype needs an observed interaction.** Use may reveal what description cannot. Ensure the artifact captures the specific moment under discussion.
- **A draft needs targeted critique.** One well-formed question outperforms an open call for comments. Specify what kind of response would change the next revision.
- **Conflicting perspectives need structured comparison.** Different users, operators, or experts expose different constraints. Compare responses against explicit criteria instead of averaging them.
- **Learning later would be expensive.** A short early review may prevent substantial rework. Recommend it when the target and the decision impact justify the interruption.
- **An assumption about the audience has never been checked.** The work may rest on a belief about who uses it and why. Test the belief against someone who fits the description.
- **The work will be handed to someone who was not consulted.** A future owner, operator, or maintainer may hold constraints nobody has asked about. Ask before the constraint becomes a defect.
- **A decision has already been made and needs a red team.** Committed choices benefit from a reviewer asked specifically to find the failure. Frame the request as adversarial so politeness does not suppress it.
- **Two internal parties disagree about what users want.** Opinion may have replaced evidence on both sides. Bring a real reaction into the argument rather than another round of assertion.

## When not to use
- **The evidence needed is already available.** Asking for a reaction to avoid deciding is delay wearing consultation's clothes.
- **The audience cannot answer the question.** Standing in one domain does not transfer to another.
- **The artifact is too vague to react to.** A response to something unformed reports the reader's imagination.
- **The decision is already irreversible.** Feedback that cannot change anything spends goodwill for nothing.
- **The missing information is factual.** Experience is the wrong instrument for a question that has a documented answer.
- **The goal itself is disputed.** A reaction to work whose purpose is unsettled measures the disagreement, not the work.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. A factual gap belongs to investigation, and a claim to be confirmed before reliance belongs to verification. Behaviour that must be measured rather than reported belongs to experiment, and an unsettled purpose belongs to goal clarification. If the caller has the evidence and is avoiding the decision, the useful category is prioritization or completion, and if the work simply needs to reach the person who will own it, use coordination.
