# Feedback

## Description
A feedback suggestion seeks a specific reaction from someone positioned to notice what the caller cannot. It names what to show, who should see it, and what question will produce a useful response. The artifact must be developed enough to reveal the relevant behavior or judgment without pretending to be final. The timing should still leave room to change the work if the response matters. The suggestion should distinguish decision-relevant signal from general approval or politeness. Use this category when another perspective can materially improve a choice, not when the caller is avoiding responsibility for making it.

## Why use / use cases
- [ ] **A user may experience an unseen point of friction.** The caller’s familiarity can hide confusion, hesitation, or unmet need. Use this category when a representative interaction can reveal behavior the caller cannot infer reliably.
- [ ] **An expert can identify a consequential flaw early.** A reviewer with relevant standing may see technical, legal, operational, or domain risks. Suggest feedback when the artifact is ready for that focused judgment and revision is still affordable.
- [ ] **Several interpretations of the work are plausible.** A reaction can show whether the intended message or value is landing. Use it when the question asks which interpretation occurs rather than whether someone generally likes the work.
- [ ] **A stakeholder must accept a tradeoff.** The caller may need to learn which cost, scope, or risk is tolerable to an affected party. Recommend feedback when the conversation can surface a decision rather than collect ceremonial approval.
- [ ] **A prototype needs an observed interaction.** Actual use may reveal issues that a verbal description cannot. Use this category when the test artifact captures the specific moment and behavior under discussion.
- [ ] **A draft needs a targeted critique.** One well-formed question can produce more useful input than an open request for comments. Suggest it when the caller can specify what kind of response would change the next revision.
- [ ] **Conflicting perspectives need a structured comparison.** Different users, operators, or experts may expose different constraints. Use feedback when the responses can be compared against explicit criteria rather than averaged into vague consensus.
- [ ] **The cost of learning later is high.** A small early conversation or review may prevent expensive rework. Recommend it when the feedback target and decision impact are clear enough to justify interrupting the current flow.

## Things to consider
- What blind spot or decision does the feedback need to address?
- Who has direct experience, authority, or exposure relevant to that question?
- What exact artifact, behavior, or scenario should they inspect?
- Which question elicits evidence instead of general approval?
- What response would change the work, and what response would not?
- Is the artifact mature enough for useful feedback but early enough to revise?
- How will conflicting or biased responses be interpreted?
- What decision and revision window will the feedback feed?

## Generation requirements

Every feedback suggestion must seek a specific reaction from a person positioned to notice something the
caller cannot reliably judge. The category exists because proximity to work destroys perspective on it:
the author knows what they meant, so they cannot see what the artifact actually communicates or where it
fails for someone encountering it cold. The requirement that follows is selection — the respondent needs
relevant experience, exposure, authority, or use, because a convenient audience produces a comfortable
answer. The material shown has to be developed enough to provoke the reaction the caller needs while
still being changeable, since feedback gathered after the decision is irreversible is theatre. The
question matters as much as the audience: broad requests for thoughts return politeness, while a focused
question returns signal. And the proposal has to say how a response becomes a change, including what to
do when responses conflict or are too sparse to read. The proposal should:

- Name the decision, artifact, audience, and question that feedback will inform.
- Choose people with relevant experience, authority, use, or exposure rather than a convenient audience.
- Show enough concrete material for an informed reaction without asking for a full review of vague work.
- Ask focused questions that distinguish signal, objection, confusion, preference, and politeness.
- Define how feedback will be recorded, compared, and connected to a change in the work.
- Protect the recipient's time, privacy, safety, and ability to disagree without pressure.
- Ask while meaningful change is still possible, not after the decision is effectively closed.
- Set a response window and a rule for acting on conflicting or sparse feedback.
- Keep the caller responsible for the decision the feedback informs.

## Alignment check

Alignment, for feedback, means the candidate borrows someone else's vantage point. The caller cannot see
their own work the way a first-time reader, an affected user, or an experienced practitioner sees it, and
this category closes that gap by putting concrete material in front of a specific person and asking a
specific question. An aligned candidate names all three: what the respondent sees, what they are being
asked, and how their answer will change the work. Asking someone to take a look is not a complete
action, because it delegates the framing to the respondent and returns whatever they happened to notice.

The second half of alignment is timing and responsibility. Feedback is only worth collecting while the
work can still move, and it informs a decision rather than making it — a candidate that uses other
people's opinions to avoid a tradeoff the caller has to own is misaligned even when the responses are
useful. A need for authoritative facts rather than reaction belongs to investigation or verification;
an outcome that is unclear in the first place belongs to goal clarification; an audience that cannot
observe what is being asked cannot supply signal; and a request made after no meaningful change remains
possible has no place. Keep the candidate here only when the live question is what response, from which
qualified person, would change the work.

## When not to use
Do not use this category when the caller needs to make a decision with evidence already available or when the requested audience cannot answer the question. Avoid it when the artifact is too vague to produce an informed reaction or when feedback would arrive after the decision is irreversible. If the missing information is factual rather than experiential, use investigation or verification; if the goal itself is disputed, use goal clarification.
