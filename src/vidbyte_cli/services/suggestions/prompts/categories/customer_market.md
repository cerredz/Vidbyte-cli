# Customer and Market

## Description

A customer-and-market suggestion identifies whom an offering should serve and which problem is worth
solving for them. It can uncover underserved audiences, new use cases, or a different market for an
existing capability. The category separates the person who uses a solution from the person who pays
for it when those roles differ. It treats demand as something to investigate through behavior,
alternatives, and willingness to change rather than as a demographic guess. A useful idea names the
problem, affected audience, and situation in which the problem becomes urgent. It also explains how
the opportunity could be tested before the business commits to a new market.

## Why use / use cases

- **A valuable group is poorly served.** People may be relying on workarounds because existing offers
  ignore their constraints. Use this category when the group and its unmet problem can be described.
- **Users and payers are different people.** The person who experiences value may not control the
  budget. Use it when the idea must account for both adoption and purchasing authority.
- **An existing capability may fit another use case.** Current work may solve a neighboring problem
  with little change. Use this lens when a plausible job and user context can be tested.
- **A market appears attractive but is unverified.** A large population or trend is not proof of
  demand. Use the category to define the smallest evidence-gathering step before investment.
- **Customers are solving the problem manually.** Repeated spreadsheets, messages, or workarounds
  reveal effort that an offer might reduce. Use it when the workaround and its cost are observable.
- **Different segments need different value.** One audience may value speed while another values
  control, trust, or compliance. Use the category when segment-specific needs would change the offer.
- **A market boundary may be too narrow or broad.** The current definition may exclude adjacent
  buyers or include people who cannot be served economically. Use this lens to test a better boundary.
- **A strategic choice needs demand evidence.** The business may need to choose which audience to
  approach first. Use it when an experiment can distinguish real demand from stated interest.

## Things to consider

- Who experiences the problem most often and most urgently?
- Who uses the solution, who pays, and who can block adoption?
- What workaround or competing alternative is used today?
- What observable behavior indicates a real unmet need?
- What job, context, and trigger define the proposed use case?
- Which segment can be served with the available capabilities and capacity?
- What willingness-to-pay or switching evidence exists?
- What smallest interview, pilot, or behavioral test could falsify the opportunity?

## When not to use

Do not use this category when the audience is already clear and the main decision concerns pricing,
message, route to purchase, or post-purchase service. Avoid demographic expansion without a concrete
problem, workaround, or demand signal. Use business and growth for a cross-cutting commercial plan
and product and experience for improving an identified user interaction.
