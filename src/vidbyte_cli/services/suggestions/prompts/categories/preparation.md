# Preparation

## Description
A preparation suggestion creates readiness for an anticipated event, transition, or demand. It joins present work to a credible trigger and to the response that trigger will require, which means both halves have to be named. The trigger supplies the deadline and the justification; the response supplies the specification of what readiness actually means here. Preparation can take the form of information, decisions, relationships, materials, access, or practice, and the right form follows from what the response will need under time pressure. The work must complete before the trigger arrives, and it must not depend on assumptions that will have decayed by then. Readiness built on a stale assumption is worse than no preparation, because it will be trusted.

The suggestion should define an observable readiness signal that can be checked close to the event, since readiness believed is not readiness demonstrated. It should identify the latest safe completion point, which is usually earlier than the trigger and is the number people misjudge. It should say which readiness assumptions are most likely to decay, and what would refresh them. It should keep any contingency proportionate to the likely disruption, because contingency planning has no natural stopping point and will expand to consume whatever attention it is given. It should say when preparation stops, so that a bounded investment does not become open-ended rehearsal. Where the trigger is too speculative to date or too diffuse to describe, the honest conclusion is that there is nothing specific to prepare for.

## Why use
Use this category when something is coming that will not leave room to improvise. The defining feature is a compressed response window: at the moment of the event, the time available is less than the time required, so the difference has to be paid in advance. That structure is common and routinely under-appreciated, because the event feels distant right up until it is not, and the cost of the surprise lands on whoever is present rather than on whoever deferred the preparation.

The category also does a second, quieter job. Preparing forces a rehearsal of the response, and rehearsal exposes gaps that planning does not: the access nobody has, the person who was never told, the step that assumes something already dismantled. Much of the value here comes from the discovery rather than from the readiness, which is why the suggestion should favour preparation that actually tests the response over preparation that merely accumulates materials for it.

Distinguish it from the categories nearest to it. Prerequisite concerns a condition that blocks work happening now, whereas preparation concerns readiness for work that will happen later; conflating them makes present blockage look like prudent foresight. Long-term directions concerns a horizon with no specific trigger, and the presence of a datable event is exactly what separates the two. Risk prevention guards against a plausible failure whether or not a scheduled event causes it, and preparation is the right category when the event is expected rather than feared. Continuation advances work already underway. Use preparation when a bounded investment now measurably reduces an expensive surprise later.

## Use cases
- **A known event has a hard response deadline.** A launch, meeting, migration, audit, or seasonal peak may leave no room to improvise. Build and check readiness before the trigger.
- **A transition will change responsibilities or conditions.** New ownership, tooling, staffing, or policy may create predictable needs. Use it when the affected people and the required readiness can be identified.
- **A likely demand could exceed capacity.** Materials, access, training, or fallbacks may be needed before volume arrives. Use it when the demand signal is credible and the preparation is proportionate.
- **A decision must be made before the event.** Waiting for the trigger may remove the safe or affordable options. Decide early when doing so preserves choice without overcommitting.
- **A relationship needs lead time.** Stakeholders, partners, reviewers, or suppliers may need notice to participate. Use it when the outreach has a concrete purpose and a date.
- **Practice would reduce response error.** A rehearsal, simulation, or runbook may expose gaps before the real situation does. Use it when the exercise tests the actual response against a readiness threshold.
- **Inputs take time to secure.** Access, inventory, data, equipment, or permissions may be unobtainable at short notice. Secure them now when that removes a known failure mode.
- **A fallback would protect the main response.** A sound plan may still be vulnerable to one predictable disruption. Recommend a proportionate contingency with a clear trigger and activation boundary.
- **The response depends on someone who may be unavailable.** A single person may hold knowledge, access, or authority needed at the moment of the event. Spread it before the event rather than during it.
- **Recovery has never been exercised.** A backup, rollback, or failover may exist on paper and never have been run. Test it while failure is optional.
- **The event will consume attention that planning needs.** Decisions made under the pressure of the event are worse than the same decisions made beforehand. Move them earlier deliberately.
- **A seasonal or cyclical pattern is approaching again.** A recurring event may have produced known problems last time. Address the specific ones rather than preparing in general.

## When not to use
- **Work cannot proceed now for want of a condition.** Present blockage is a dependency, not future readiness.
- **The trigger is too speculative to date.** Preparing for an unspecified event has no deadline and no stopping point.
- **No readiness signal can be defined.** Readiness that cannot be checked will be assumed.
- **The work is already underway and needs its next step.** Momentum does not require rehearsal.
- **The concern is a broad future horizon.** Without a specific trigger, the argument is about direction.
- **The contingency exceeds the disruption.** Protection more expensive than the failure is a loss taken deliberately.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. A blocking condition belongs to prerequisite, and an advancing plan belongs to continuation. A horizon without a trigger belongs to long-term directions, and a plausible failure needing a proportionate guard belongs to risk prevention. If the event's likelihood or shape is genuinely unknown, use investigation before preparing, and if several possible preparations compete for the same capacity, use prioritization.
