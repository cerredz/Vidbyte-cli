# Preparation

## Description
A preparation suggestion creates readiness for an anticipated event, transition, or demand. It connects present work to a credible trigger and the response that will be needed when it arrives. Preparation may involve information, decisions, relationships, materials, access, or practice. The recommendation should finish before the trigger without relying on assumptions that will decay or become irrelevant. It should include a readiness signal that can be checked near the event. Use this category when a bounded investment can reduce expensive surprise without becoming an endless rehearsal.

## Why use / use cases
- [ ] **A known event has a hard response deadline.** A launch, meeting, migration, audit, or seasonal demand may leave no time to improvise. Use this category when readiness can be built and checked before the trigger.
- [ ] **A transition will change responsibilities or conditions.** New ownership, tooling, staffing, or policy may create predictable needs. Suggest preparation when the affected people and required readiness are identifiable.
- [ ] **A likely demand could overwhelm current capacity.** The caller may need materials, access, training, or fallback options before volume arrives. Use it when the demand signal is credible and preparation can be proportionate.
- [ ] **A critical decision must be made before the event.** Waiting until the trigger may remove safe or affordable choices. Recommend preparation when deciding early preserves options without overcommitting.
- [ ] **A relationship needs lead time.** Stakeholders, partners, reviewers, or suppliers may need advance notice to participate. Use this category when the outreach has a concrete purpose and timing.
- [ ] **Practice can reduce response error.** A rehearsal, simulation, or runbook may expose gaps before the real situation. Suggest it when the exercise tests the actual response and has a clear readiness threshold.
- [ ] **Inputs or materials take time to secure.** Access, inventory, data, equipment, or permissions may be unavailable at the last moment. Use preparation when securing them now reduces a known failure mode.
- [ ] **A fallback can protect the main response.** The primary plan may be sound but vulnerable to one predictable disruption. Recommend a proportionate contingency when its trigger and activation boundary are clear.

## Things to consider
- What event, transition, or demand triggers the need for readiness?
- How credible, consequential, and time-bound is the trigger?
- What must be ready in information, access, people, materials, decisions, or practice?
- Which readiness assumptions may decay before the event?
- What is the latest safe completion point?
- What observable test or signal proves readiness?
- What contingency is proportionate to the likely disruption?
- When should preparation stop so it does not become rehearsal without purpose?

## Generation requirements

Every preparation suggestion must make the caller ready for a credible future event, transition, or
demand before the cost of surprise arrives. The proposal should:

- Name the trigger, date, condition, audience, or transition that makes readiness relevant.
- Identify the response that must be possible when the trigger occurs and what would make it fail.
- Prepare information, decisions, relationships, materials, capability, access, or practice that will remain usable.
- Finish before the trigger and account for decay, ownership, dependencies, and last responsible timing.
- Distinguish readiness work from a prerequisite that blocks current work or a broad long-term direction.
- Use supplied context to establish the trigger and likely response; label timing or demand assumptions.
- Define a readiness signal another person can inspect without relying on confidence or intent.
- Avoid endless rehearsal, speculative stockpiling, or preparation that creates more work than the event warrants.
- Include a review or refresh condition when information, access, or capacity may expire.
- Keep readiness before a credible trigger as the primary mechanism; route missing requirements and future strategy elsewhere.

## Candidate shape

Shape the candidate as a readiness package tied to a future trigger. The reader should know what must
be ready, by when, how readiness is checked, and what happens if the trigger changes.

- In the **summary**, name the trigger, expected response, readiness gap, and consequence of surprise.
- In the **action sequence**, identify durable preparations, assign owners, finish before the trigger, and run a readiness check.
- In **decision points**, choose preparation scope, timing, refresh interval, owner, escalation, and stop condition.
- In **considerations**, cover decay, access, dependencies, capacity, rehearsal value, privacy, cost, and distraction.
- In **dependencies**, name people, materials, data, permissions, practice, or decisions required before the event.
- In **evidence references**, cite the trigger, prior event, stated demand, or known response; do not cite vague possibility as urgency.
- In **assumptions**, label trigger likelihood and response requirements with a review or refresh signal.
- In the **completion criterion**, require a checkable readiness state before the trigger and a plan for stale preparation.
- If the condition is required for work that cannot proceed now, use prerequisite.

## Valid suggestion directions

Use this category for readiness moves:

- Prepare an artifact, decision, contact, or access needed at a known future moment.
- Rehearse a high-consequence response with a bounded scenario and stopping point.
- Validate capacity, materials, staffing, or support before a credible demand increase.
- Establish a communication, escalation, or recovery plan before an event.
- Precompute information or choices that will be expensive under time pressure.
- Practice a skill whose value depends on being usable before the trigger arrives.
- Refresh a stale plan, credential, dependency, or relationship before it fails.
- Run a readiness review with a named owner and observable pass condition.
- Preserve options while avoiding speculative preparation beyond the event's credible need.

## Alignment check

The candidate is aligned when present work prepares a credible future response and has a readiness
signal before the trigger. It should reduce expensive surprise without becoming an unbounded rehearsal.
The trigger and timing are as important as the artifact being prepared.

Reject or reroute candidates that:

- Are required before current work can proceed; use prerequisite.
- Advance an accepted current plan; use continuation.
- Develop a broad future direction with no specific trigger; use long-term directions.
- Gather facts without preparing a response; use investigation.
- Prepare for an event too speculative to justify cost or maintenance.

The primary decision must be what readiness is worth completing before which credible trigger. The
candidate should identify who checks readiness and what stale preparation must be refreshed or discarded.

## When not to use
Do not use this category for a missing condition required by work that cannot proceed now; use prerequisite instead. Avoid it when the event is too speculative to justify preparation or when no readiness signal can be defined. If the work is already underway and simply needs its next step, use continuation; if the concern is a broad future direction, use long-term directions.
