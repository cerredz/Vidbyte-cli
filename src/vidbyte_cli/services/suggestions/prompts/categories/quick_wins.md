# Quick Wins

## Description
A quick-win suggestion offers a small, inexpensive action with a useful near-term payoff. It is allowed to sit outside the main plan, which is what distinguishes it from continuation, provided its value is clear and its distraction cost is genuinely low. The payoff must be concrete — time saved, friction removed, evidence created, a loop closed — rather than merely pleasant or easy to count. The action must not depend on a chain of uncertain follow-up work, because a small first step towards an uncertain project is not a quick win but the cheapest part of a large commitment. Cheapness alone is never the argument, since an action can be easy and worth nothing. What justifies the category is a real benefit that arrives soon, at a cost small enough that it does not compete with anything important.

The suggestion should state the specific near-term payoff and the observation by which it will be recognized, because unverified small improvements accumulate into unverified small clutter. It should account for the time, attention, money, and coordination the action requires, and coordination is the one most often omitted and most often decisive. It should say what higher-priority work could be displaced, since the danger of this category is not any single suggestion but the aggregate. It should confirm that the dependencies and follow-up steps really are absent rather than merely unexamined. It should say how soon the payoff appears, and it should give a stopping point if the benefit does not materialize. An action whose benefit cannot be observed within a short horizon belongs to a different category.

## Why use
Use this category when there is a genuine asymmetry between cost and benefit at small scale. Most work is not like this; most work costs roughly what it returns. The exceptions are worth surfacing precisely because they are exceptions, and because they are usually invisible to whoever is inside the main effort, who has correctly learned to protect their attention from anything not on the plan. A suggestion agent can see the cheap high-return action that the person cannot afford to go looking for.

The category also has a legitimate role after a stall. Work that has been paused for a long time accumulates an activation cost that grows with the pause, and a small completed action can reduce it in a way that further planning cannot. That argument is real, and it is also the argument most easily abused, so the suggestion must require that the completed action produces progress or evidence rather than only relief. A quick win whose entire benefit is feeling better is not one.

Keep the boundary against its neighbours sharp, because this category attracts misuse. If the action is required for the main goal, it belongs to continuation, completion, or prerequisite regardless of how small it is; labelling required work a quick win understates its importance. Adjacent opportunity argues from a closing window on optional value, which is a timing claim rather than a cost-benefit one. Simplification removes accumulated complexity, which may be large. Leverage builds something reused many times, where a quick win pays off once. Use quick wins when the action is small, optional, soon-rewarding, and not the start of something larger.

## Use cases
- **A small fix removes recurring friction.** Time or clarity may be lost to a simple repeated annoyance. Use it when the fix has a direct payoff and needs no broad redesign.
- **A cheap action creates immediate evidence.** A measurement, draft, request, or small comparison may clarify a larger choice. Suggest it when the evidence is useful even if nothing follows.
- **A better default would improve consistency.** A reminder, template, setting, or written-down choice may prevent repeated small errors. Use it when the default is safe and easy to reverse.
- **Momentum matters after a stall.** A modest completed action can make stalled work concrete again. Recommend it only when completion produces progress rather than relief.
- **A near-term benefit funds later work.** A small improvement may save time, reduce cost, or demonstrate demand. Use it when the payoff appears within a short horizon.
- **An action is unusually cheap right now.** Existing access, context, or setup may reduce the cost temporarily. Use it only while the action stays genuinely bounded.
- **A reversible cleanup improves the current state.** Removing stale clutter, fixing a label, or closing a minor loop may reduce future confusion. Use it when a specific workflow or user benefits.
- **A minimal exposure would test willingness or usability.** A small trial may teach enough before further investment. Recommend it when the signal is meaningful and the stopping point explicit.
- **A five-minute task has been deferred repeatedly.** Work that costs more to keep tracking than to finish is pure overhead. Close it, and stop carrying it.
- **A known annoyance affects several people daily.** A small shared irritation multiplied across a team is not small. Fix it when the aggregate is measurable.
- **A missing piece of documentation is answering itself repeatedly.** The same question asked weekly has a cheap permanent answer. Write it once, where people already look.
- **An automatable manual step is short but frequent.** High frequency turns a trivial cost into a real one. Automate it when the automation is itself trivial.

## When not to use
- **The action is required for the main goal.** Necessary work mislabelled as optional gets deprioritized against its importance.
- **The only virtue is that it is easy.** Cheapness without payoff is activity.
- **Follow-up work is large and uncertain.** A small first step into a big commitment is not a small commitment.
- **The payoff cannot be observed soon.** A benefit with no near horizon cannot be checked and will be assumed.
- **Attention is already scarce and contested.** Adding optional work to an overloaded queue makes the queue worse.
- **The upside is large with meaningful exposure.** A consequential opportunity deserves to be argued at its real size.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. Required work belongs to continuation, completion, or prerequisite, and contested attention belongs to prioritization. A large, uncertain, high-upside commitment belongs to big bets, and optional value with a closing window belongs to adjacent opportunity. Removing accumulated complexity belongs to simplification, and building something reused repeatedly belongs to leverage. If the small action is really a bounded test of an open question, use experiment so its result gets a decision rule.
