# Investigation

## Description
An investigation gathers missing knowledge that a consequential action or decision depends on. It begins with a precise question and ends when the evidence available is sufficient for the decision at hand, which is a much lower bar than sufficient in general. The distinguishing feature is that the answer already exists somewhere and needs to be found rather than produced, which is what separates this category from experiment. Sources should be chosen for authority, accessibility, currency, and relevance, in proportion to what the decision is worth. More information is not automatically better, and information that cannot change the decision is a cost with no return. An investigation without a named decision behind it has no way to know when it is done.

The suggestion should say what evidence would be sufficient rather than merely interesting, because that distinction is the only defence against research that expands to fill available time. It should say how source quality, definitions, and conflicts will be evaluated, since disagreement between sources is usually a definitional problem rather than a factual one. It should exclude information with low decision value explicitly, as the instinct to be thorough is what turns a two-hour question into a two-week one. It should include a stopping rule and a synthesis format, so that what was learned survives as something other than open tabs. It should say how the findings will change the next action or the caller's confidence, and if neither would move, the investigation should not be run. Where the stakes are low and the action is reversible, acting is frequently the cheaper way to learn.

## Why use
Use this category when information is the binding input to the next choice — not execution, not persuasion, not alignment, but knowing something that is currently unknown. This is worth checking deliberately, because the felt experience of being blocked by uncertainty is very similar to the felt experience of being blocked by indecision, and only one of them is helped by research. When it genuinely is information, a short, targeted inquiry is among the highest-return actions available, since it can prevent a commitment made on a false premise.

The category also exists to discipline research that has stopped being useful. Open-ended reading feels productive, produces a legible artifact of effort, and defers the decision indefinitely, which makes it the most comfortable form of avoidance available to a capable person. Requiring a question, a source set, a sufficiency threshold, and a stopping rule converts that into bounded work. The requirement to name what would change as a result is the sharpest of these, because it fails immediately for investigations whose real purpose is delay.

Distinguish it from its neighbours. Experiment intervenes in the world to produce evidence that does not yet exist, and intervening is the wrong move whenever the answer can simply be found. Verification checks a specific claim believed true against a known acceptance condition, which is narrower and usually faster. Learning builds a durable capability rather than answering one question, and the two are confused whenever a question is large enough to feel like a subject. Feedback collects a judgment or an experience from a person, which is the right instrument when the missing input is not a fact. Use investigation when the fact exists, matters, and is findable.

## Use cases
- **A consequential decision rests on a missing fact.** Acting without it may create material cost, risk, or rework. Gather only the evidence that bears on the decision.
- **Sources disagree about an important claim.** Conflicting information may change the route or the confidence behind it. Compare source quality, definitions, timing, and evidence rather than counting opinions.
- **The current state is not known.** An inventory, baseline, dependency map, or history may be missing. Use it when the snapshot will determine a concrete action or allocation.
- **A constraint or requirement is unclear.** Policy, law, contract, platform behaviour, or stakeholder need may govern what is possible. Identify the governing source and apply it.
- **A pattern needs explanation before intervention.** Repeated behaviour may have several candidate causes. Gather discriminating evidence rather than choosing a remedy immediately.
- **An external option must be compared.** Vendors, methods, markets, or practices may differ on criteria that matter. Bound the comparison frame and set the decision threshold in advance.
- **The cost of being wrong is high.** A short evidence-gathering step may protect a much larger commitment. Use it when the inquiry materially lowers uncertainty before exposure grows.
- **Research is running without a stopping rule.** Open-ended reading can defer action while feeling productive. Impose an explicit question, source set, synthesis format, and evidence threshold.
- **A number is being assumed rather than known.** Cost, volume, latency, or capacity may have entered the plan as a guess. Establish the real figure when the plan is sensitive to it.
- **Prior work on this problem may already exist.** Someone inside or outside the organization may have answered this already. Check before rediscovering it at full price.
- **A claimed constraint may not be real.** A rule, a limit, or an impossibility may be folklore rather than policy. Find the source, and read it.
- **The decision needs history, not analysis.** Understanding why something is the way it is may be the only route to changing it safely. Recover the rationale before proposing a replacement.

## When not to use
- **The evidence is already available.** Re-gathering what is known spends time to produce nothing.
- **The answer must be produced rather than found.** Where no source can hold it, intervention is the only route.
- **No plausible finding would change the decision.** Research that cannot move the choice is deferral.
- **The claim has a known acceptance condition.** A targeted check is faster than an inquiry.
- **The gap is a capability, not a fact.** Knowing about something is not the same as being able to do it.
- **The action is cheap and reversible.** Doing it may answer the question faster than reading about it.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. Producing new evidence by intervening belongs to experiment, and confirming a specific claim belongs to verification. Building a durable capability belongs to learning, and collecting a person's judgment or experience belongs to feedback. A cheap reversible action that would settle the matter belongs to immediate next steps, and an unclear outcome that makes every finding ambiguous belongs to goal clarification.
