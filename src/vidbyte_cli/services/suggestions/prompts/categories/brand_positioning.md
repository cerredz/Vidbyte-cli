# Brand and Positioning

## Description

A brand-and-positioning suggestion clarifies what a business stands for and why a person should choose
it over alternatives. It shapes the promise, associations, message, reputation, and place an offering
occupies in a customer's mental shortlist. The category is about a believable difference, not a slogan
or visual refresh by itself. A strong idea identifies the comparison set and the customer situation in
which the difference matters. It connects the claim to proof that the business can repeatedly provide.
It should make the intended memory and the evidence for that memory concrete enough to test.

## Why use / use cases

- **People cannot explain why the offering is different.** Unclear meaning makes a good product look
  interchangeable. Use this lens when a sharper promise could change consideration.
- **The business is compared with the wrong alternatives.** Customers may judge it against a cheaper,
  riskier, or less relevant option. Use it when reframing the comparison would alter choice.
- **A new audience needs a credible reason to care.** Existing language may describe features that do
  not match the audience's priorities. Use the category when a value promise can be tied to proof.
- **The current promise is broader than delivery capability.** Ambitious claims can create skepticism
  or inconsistent experiences. Use it to narrow the position to a difference the business can show.
- **A product change creates a new identity question.** New capabilities may confuse what the business
  is for. Use this lens when the change affects the promise, not only the interface.
- **Trust or reputation is limiting adoption.** People may hesitate because the business is associated
  with an unwanted risk or tradeoff. Use the category when proof, behavior, or message could repair it.
- **A message works in one channel but not another.** The core position may need clearer expression
  without changing its meaning. Use it when consistency and translation are the problem.
- **A positioning choice can be tested.** The business may need to compare two clear promises before
  committing. Use this lens when a message, landing page, or sales conversation can reveal preference.

## Things to consider

- What should the intended customer remember after the interaction?
- Which alternatives will the customer compare, and on what dimension?
- What specific difference matters in the customer's decision context?
- What evidence proves the business can deliver that difference?
- Which audience is included, and which audience is intentionally not targeted?
- Could the claim create expectations the product or service cannot meet?
- How would the position sound across product, sales, support, and partner touchpoints?
- What message or behavior test could distinguish the position from attractive wording alone?

## Generation requirements

Every brand-and-positioning suggestion must change or test how a specific audience understands,
compares, remembers, or trusts the offering. Positioning is a choice about meaning rather than
decoration: it decides which comparison the offering is judged inside, what it promises, and what it
therefore has to refuse to promise. That last part carries most of the weight, because a position that
excludes nothing cannot differentiate anything and gives the organization no way to make consistent
choices. The claim also has to be provable — a promise the business can repeatedly deliver, backed by
something in the product, service, behavior, or operating model — since an unsupported claim converts
attention into disappointment and costs more trust than it buys. Positions live in customer language,
not internal strategy vocabulary, and they only exist once they change a real artifact, conversation, or
touchpoint. Because meaning is established in the audience rather than declared by the business, it has
to be tested rather than announced. The proposal should:

- Name the audience, decision context, and comparison set in which the position must matter.
- State the intended association, promise, or remembered difference in language a customer could
  recognize without internal strategy vocabulary.
- Explain why that difference is meaningful to the audience and distinct from the alternatives they
  would actually consider.
- Connect the claim to proof in the product, service, behavior, evidence, or operating model that the
  business can repeatedly provide.
- Identify what the position excludes, de-emphasizes, or refuses to promise so expectations stay
  credible.
- Show how the position would change a concrete artifact, conversation, behavior, or touchpoint rather
  than stopping at a slogan or visual direction.
- Propose a bounded message, recall, preference, comprehension, or behavior test before declaring the
  position established.
- Account for delivery consistency across product, sales, support, partners, and public claims.
- Name the position it displaces, since an audience already believes something about the offering.

## Alignment check

Alignment, for brand and positioning, means the candidate changes what the offering means to someone.
A person deciding between options carries an impression — what this thing is for, who it is for, what it
promises, how far it can be trusted, what it sits next to in their mind — and that impression drives the
choice before any feature comparison happens. An aligned candidate names the audience and the comparison
set they are actually choosing within, states the difference it wants remembered in language that
audience would use, and points at the proof that makes the claim survivable. It also names what the
position gives up, because a promise that excludes nothing distinguishes nothing.

The second half of alignment is causal direction. A positioning candidate may use a page, a campaign, a
sales script, a product behavior, or a proof artifact as its instrument, but the intended change has to
be in how customers interpret and choose, not merely that a new artifact now exists. Adding a channel,
partnership, or checkout improvement without changing meaning belongs to distribution and sales; changing
price, packaging, or value capture belongs to business model and monetization; establishing an unknown
audience or problem before a position can be chosen belongs to customer and market; a usability problem
belongs to product and experience; post-purchase onboarding, support, or renewal belongs to customer
relationship and service; and a general growth plan with positioning as one ingredient belongs to
business and growth. Keep the candidate here only when a change in customer meaning, comparison,
expectation, or trust is the decision that determines the other actions.

## Weak suggestion patterns

- “Refresh the brand” without an audience, comparison, promise, proof, or test.
- A slogan that sounds distinctive but changes no customer choice or expectation.
- A competitor claim unsupported by supplied evidence or a defined comparison set.
- A visual redesign treated as proof that customers will remember or trust the offering.
- A message test measured only by internal approval or raw attention instead of customer response.

## When not to use

Do not use this category for a visual identity exercise without a choice, comparison, or trust
hypothesis. Avoid it when the main problem is channel access, pricing, or an interaction that users
cannot complete. Use customer and market when the audience or problem is unknown, and business and
growth when the proposal intentionally spans positioning with other growth mechanisms.
