# Brand and Positioning

## Description

A brand-and-positioning suggestion clarifies what a business stands for and why a person should choose
it over alternatives. It shapes the promise, associations, message, reputation, and place an offering
occupies in a customer's mental shortlist. The category is about a believable difference, not a slogan
or visual refresh by itself. A strong idea identifies the comparison set and the customer situation in
which the difference matters. It connects the claim to proof that the business can repeatedly provide.
It should make the intended memory and the evidence for that memory concrete enough to test.

## Why use / use cases

- **People cannot explain why the offering is different.** Unclear meaning makes a good product look
  interchangeable. Use this lens when a sharper promise could change consideration.
- **The business is compared with the wrong alternatives.** Customers may judge it against a cheaper,
  riskier, or less relevant option. Use it when reframing the comparison would alter choice.
- **A new audience needs a credible reason to care.** Existing language may describe features that do
  not match the audience's priorities. Use the category when a value promise can be tied to proof.
- **The current promise is broader than delivery capability.** Ambitious claims can create skepticism
  or inconsistent experiences. Use it to narrow the position to a difference the business can show.
- **A product change creates a new identity question.** New capabilities may confuse what the business
  is for. Use this lens when the change affects the promise, not only the interface.
- **Trust or reputation is limiting adoption.** People may hesitate because the business is associated
  with an unwanted risk or tradeoff. Use the category when proof, behavior, or message could repair it.
- **A message works in one channel but not another.** The core position may need clearer expression
  without changing its meaning. Use it when consistency and translation are the problem.
- **A positioning choice can be tested.** The business may need to compare two clear promises before
  committing. Use this lens when a message, landing page, or sales conversation can reveal preference.

## Generation requirements

Every brand-and-positioning suggestion must change or test how a specific audience understands,
compares, remembers, or trusts the offering. Treat positioning as a choice about meaning and
expectation, not as decoration or clever copy. The proposal should:

- Name the audience, decision context, and comparison set in which the position must matter.
- State the intended association, promise, or remembered difference in language a customer could
  recognize without internal strategy vocabulary.
- Explain why that difference is meaningful to the audience and distinct from the alternatives they
  would actually consider.
- Connect the claim to proof in the product, service, behavior, evidence, or operating model that
  the business can repeatedly provide.
- Identify what the position excludes, de-emphasizes, or refuses to promise so expectations remain
  credible and the organization can make consistent choices.
- Show how the position would change a concrete artifact, conversation, behavior, or touchpoint,
  rather than stopping at a slogan or visual direction.
- Use supplied evidence about customer language, trust, alternatives, or behavior; label unfamiliar
  perceptions and competitive assumptions instead of inventing them.
- Propose a bounded message, recall, preference, comprehension, or behavior test before declaring
  that the position has been established.
- Account for delivery consistency across product, sales, support, partners, and public claims.
- Keep meaning, comparison, and trust as the primary mechanism; route audience discovery, channel
  access, pricing, and interaction design to their focused categories.

## Candidate shape

Shape the candidate as a positioning hypothesis that becomes visible in a concrete customer-facing
artifact and can be evaluated. Another agent should be able to identify the audience, the old frame,
the proposed frame, the proof, and the signal that would justify keeping it.

- In the **summary**, state who should remember what difference, compared with which alternative, in
  which decision situation.
- In the **action sequence**, gather existing language and evidence, formulate the position, express
  it in one or more artifacts, expose it to a bounded audience, and evaluate the response.
- In **decision points**, choose the audience, comparison frame, promise, proof, exclusions, tone,
  touchpoint translation, and test design.
- In **considerations**, cover relevance, distinctness, credibility, delivery capability, trust,
  inclusion, consistency, competitive response, and the risk of overclaiming.
- In **dependencies**, name customer language, a proof artifact, an owner of the message, access to
  a test audience, or product and service changes needed to make the promise true.
- In **evidence references**, cite observed wording, comparisons, objections, outcomes, or behavior;
  do not treat internal preference for a phrase as customer evidence.
- In **assumptions**, identify unknown associations, recall, preference, or proof strength and pair
  each one with a way to observe or challenge it.
- In the **completion criterion**, require a tested artifact, a defined customer response, and a
  decision to keep, revise, narrow, or reject the position.
- If the business cannot credibly deliver the claim, make proof-building or promise-narrowing part
  of the suggestion instead of recommending louder communication.

## Valid suggestion directions

Use this category to vary the meaning and proof mechanism while keeping the central change in customer
interpretation or choice. A direction should produce an actionable positioning hypothesis:

- Narrow the position around an audience whose priorities are poorly served by the current frame.
- Reframe the comparison from a familiar incumbent to the costly workaround or risk customers face.
- Replace a broad feature claim with a specific outcome, promise, or decision advantage.
- Make a real operational, product, service, or evidence advantage easier to remember and repeat.
- Repair a trust problem by pairing a narrower claim with visible behavior, proof, or recovery.
- Define what the brand will not promise so a credible position can guide tradeoffs.
- Translate one position consistently across a landing page, sales conversation, product, and support.
- Test two distinct positions with recall, comprehension, preference, or qualified behavior measures.
- Reposition after a product change when the old identity no longer explains the customer value.
- Clarify category membership when customers compare the offering with the wrong alternatives.
- Build a proof library that makes a recurring claim easier for customers and staff to substantiate.
- Separate a durable position from a campaign, slogan, visual refresh, or temporary promotion.

## Evidence and assumptions

Do not invent what customers remember, how competitors are perceived, or which message will convert.
Customer quotes, objections, comparison behavior, outcomes, and repeated delivery evidence are more
useful than internal language preferences. Treat a proposed position as a hypothesis until an
audience can recognize it, find it relevant, and connect it to credible proof. When perception data
is absent, recommend a small message or conversation test and state which result would weaken the
position. Do not use a market-size claim, a new logo, or a successful internal workshop as proof that
the offering occupies a distinct place in a customer's mind.

## Things to consider

- What should the intended customer remember after the interaction?
- Which alternatives will the customer compare, and on what dimension?
- What specific difference matters in the customer's decision context?
- What evidence proves the business can deliver that difference?
- Which audience is included, and which audience is intentionally not targeted?
- Could the claim create expectations the product or service cannot meet?
- How would the position sound across product, sales, support, and partner touchpoints?
- What message or behavior test could distinguish the position from attractive wording alone?

## Alignment check

The candidate is aligned when its primary mechanism changes the remembered meaning, comparison,
expectation, or trust attached to an offering for a named audience. A positioning suggestion may use
a page, campaign, sales script, product behavior, or proof artifact, but the intended causal change
must be how customers interpret or choose the offering. A polished artifact without a positioning
hypothesis is not enough.

Reject or reroute candidates that:

- Add a channel, partnership, sales motion, or checkout improvement without changing the offer's
  meaning; use distribution and sales.
- Change price, packaging, payment timing, or value capture; use business model and monetization.
- Define an unknown audience, problem, or market before a meaningful position can be chosen; use
  customer and market.
- Add a feature or redesign an interaction where usability, not interpretation, is the primary issue;
  use product and experience.
- Improve onboarding, support, renewal, or service recovery after purchase; use customer relationship
  and service.
- Combine several commercial mechanisms into a general growth plan without positioning as the core
  decision; use business and growth.

When several mechanisms appear together, keep the candidate here only when a change in customer
meaning, comparison, expectation, or trust is the decision that determines the other actions. A new
artifact is evidence of execution, not proof of positioning; the candidate still needs a credible
claim, supporting proof, and a response that would change the chosen position.

## Weak suggestion patterns

- “Refresh the brand” without an audience, comparison, promise, proof, or test.
- A slogan that sounds distinctive but changes no customer choice or expectation.
- A competitor claim unsupported by supplied evidence or a defined comparison set.
- A visual redesign treated as proof that customers will remember or trust the offering.
- A message test measured only by internal approval or raw attention instead of customer response.

## When not to use

Do not use this category for a visual identity exercise without a choice, comparison, or trust
hypothesis. Avoid it when the main problem is channel access, pricing, or an interaction that users
cannot complete. Use customer and market when the audience or problem is unknown, and business and
growth when the proposal intentionally spans positioning with other growth mechanisms.
