# Cross-Domain

## Description
A cross-domain suggestion transfers a working mechanism from another field into the current problem. What is borrowed is causal structure — a pattern, a constraint, a practice, or a method — and not a memorable comparison. The source field must have evidence that the mechanism works in its own context, because an untested idea from elsewhere is simply an untested idea. The mapping has to be stated in both directions: which elements correspond to the current actors, constraints, and outcomes, and where the correspondence breaks because the contexts differ. That second half is what separates a transfer from an analogy, and it is the half most often omitted. A comparison that cannot say where it fails cannot be relied on where it holds.

The suggestion should separate the portable principle from the domain-specific ritual around it, since most practices carry both and only one of them travels. It should name the new assumptions and risks that arrive with the transfer, because borrowed mechanisms bring borrowed failure modes. It should propose the smallest trial capable of testing whether the causal relationship survives the context change, rather than advocating adoption on the strength of the resemblance. It should say what result would justify adapting the transfer, narrowing it, or abandoning it, so the trial can actually conclude. Where the current problem is not defined well enough to compare contexts responsibly, the transfer cannot be evaluated and should not be proposed. A suggestion that only offers vocabulary from another field has moved words rather than mechanisms.

## Why use
Use this category when a field has been working on a problem long enough to exhaust its own solution family, and when another discipline has plausibly met the same structural problem under a different name. Queues, incentives, feedback loops, contention, learning curves, and coordination costs recur across domains with quite different surface descriptions, and the accumulated knowledge in one field is frequently decades ahead of another's on exactly the mechanism that matters. Borrowing it is cheaper than rediscovering it, and the discount is the argument.

The category also does specific work against a failure that generic idea generation cannot address. Creative exploration widens the option space but draws its options from what is already imaginable within the current frame. A transfer imports something the frame does not contain, which is a different and often larger move. The discipline the category adds — name the mechanism, show the source evidence, map the correspondence, mark where it breaks — is what keeps this from degenerating into decorative metaphor, which is what happens to most cross-domain suggestions in practice.

Distinguish it carefully from its neighbours. Creative exploration varies an assumption to generate distinct options without claiming that any of them has worked before. Reframing changes how the problem is understood using no external source at all. Learning builds a capability the caller lacks, which may be needed to execute a transfer but is not itself one. Alternative compares routes already available in the current field. Use cross-domain only when there is a real source, a real mechanism, and a mapping specific enough to be wrong.

## Use cases
- **The field keeps producing the same solution family.** A parallel discipline may have solved a structurally similar problem by another mechanism. Use the category when the borrowed mechanism would change the design space rather than decorate it.
- **A process has a recognizable analogue elsewhere.** Queues, incentives, feedback loops, learning, and coordination appear across many domains. Suggest the transfer when the underlying relationship matters more than the surface resemblance.
- **A mature practice could shorten discovery.** Another field may have established ways of observing, sequencing, training, or controlling a comparable system. Use it when the practice can be adapted without pretending the contexts are identical.
- **Local vocabulary is obstructing the problem.** A source field may offer a more productive way to name the mechanism or its boundary. Recommend it when the new naming leads to a concrete question or action.
- **A constraint resembles one handled well elsewhere.** Limited capacity, trust, attention, or reliability may have established treatments in another domain. Make the constraint mapping explicit rather than gesturing at similarity.
- **A cheap probe could test an uncertain transfer.** The mapping may be plausible and unproven while the trial is inexpensive. Suggest it when the trial tests the causal mechanism rather than the surface effect.
- **A practice is portable at the level of principle.** The original method may contain a reusable rule wrapped in context-bound ritual. Extract the principle and say explicitly which details are being discarded.
- **Another field exposes a neglected failure mode.** Comparison may surface an assumption the current field never had reason to question. Recommend the transfer when it improves prevention, measurement, or choice.
- **A measurement problem has been solved elsewhere.** Another discipline may already know how to observe the quantity that is currently being guessed at. Use it when the instrument, not the idea, is what transfers.
- **A regulated or safety-critical field has a relevant discipline.** Fields with severe consequences often have rigorous practices for review, staging, or rollback. Borrow the practice when the consequence profile genuinely justifies it.
- **An incentive structure keeps producing the wrong behaviour.** Economics, game theory, and mechanism design offer well-studied treatments for this. Suggest the specific mechanism, and say what it assumes about the actors.
- **A scaling behaviour is already characterized elsewhere.** Another domain may know how the relevant quantity behaves as size grows. Use the known curve rather than discovering it empirically.

## When not to use
- **The comparison is decorative.** A metaphor that changes no decision costs attention and returns nothing.
- **No source mechanism can be named.** Generic best practice with no origin is not a transfer.
- **The current problem is too vague to map.** Contexts cannot be compared responsibly when one of them is undefined.
- **The source evidence does not exist.** Borrowing an untested idea imports the risk without the discount.
- **Where the analogy breaks cannot be stated.** An unfalsifiable mapping will be trusted exactly where it fails.
- **The method is already adopted here.** Applying something established is execution, not transfer.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. Generating possibilities without an external source is creative exploration, and changing the interpretation of the problem is reframing. Applying an already-adopted method is continuation or preparation, and acquiring the skill to apply it is learning. Comparing routes available within the current field is alternative, and testing whether a specific borrowed mechanism holds here is experiment. If the problem is not yet defined well enough to compare anything, use goal clarification first.
