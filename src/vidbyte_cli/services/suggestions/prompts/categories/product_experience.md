# Product and Experience

## Description
A product-and-experience suggestion improves what people can use and how using it feels or works. It starts from a user, a moment, and a friction or desired outcome, never from a feature label, because a feature name describes what would be built rather than what would change for anyone. Naming the person and the moment is what makes the suggestion evaluable, since the same change is an improvement in one context and a burden in another. The proposal has to balance usefulness, clarity, accessibility, trust, and the cost of the complexity it adds, as every addition is paid for by everyone who did not need it. A strong suggestion says how people would recognize the change as better in behaviour or outcome rather than in opinion. Where that recognition cannot be described, there is no way to tell afterwards whether the change helped.

The suggestion should say what evidence supports the friction rather than assuming it, because designers and builders are unreliable witnesses to their own products. It should name which steps, language, defaults, or feedback create the current burden, since the burden is usually specific and the instinct is to redesign broadly. It should address accessibility, privacy, trust, and recovery needs explicitly, as these are the dimensions most often discovered late and most expensive to retrofit. It should say who gains, who loses, and who stays unserved, because experience changes redistribute effort rather than only removing it. It should propose the smallest representative interaction that could test the improvement before broader scope is committed. A prototype that exposes the actual moment answers more than a complete build of the wrong thing.

## Why use
Use this category when the gap between what a system can do and what people can actually get from it is where the value is being lost. That gap is large in most products and is systematically underestimated, because the people building the system experience none of it. Capability is visible internally and experience is not, so improvements accrue to the former until someone deliberately argues for the latter. This category is that argument, and it has to be made in terms of a specific person at a specific moment or it will lose to any concrete feature request.

The category also disciplines a kind of proposal that is otherwise unfalsifiable. Making something better, cleaner, simpler, or more intuitive cannot be evaluated, accepted, or rejected, and suggestions in that form absorb effort without accumulating knowledge. Requiring an observable behaviour change converts the proposal into something testable, which in turn allows a small prototype to settle a question that would otherwise be decided by whoever argues most persistently.

Distinguish it from the categories nearest to it. Business and growth argues from a commercial mechanism such as acquisition or retention, and while experience changes often drive those, the argument here is from the interaction itself. Technical possibilities concerns what the technology could make better or newly feasible, which is a capability claim rather than a use claim. Simplification removes complexity wherever it has accumulated, including in places no user ever touches. Feedback obtains a reaction and is the right prior step when the friction is suspected rather than evidenced. Use product and experience when a nameable person, at a nameable moment, would behave differently because of the change.

## Use cases
- **Users struggle at a specific moment.** A task, decision, or transition may contain friction that prevents people from reaching value. Use it when the moment and the affected users can be observed.
- **A capability exists but its value is invisible.** People may not discover, understand, or trust what is already available. Improve the interaction when that would change real use.
- **The flow creates avoidable effort.** Extra steps, confusing language, or poor defaults may obstruct a meaningful outcome. Reduce them without removing necessary safeguards.
- **An accessibility gap excludes people.** Different abilities, devices, languages, or contexts may be unsupported. Specify the affected experience and the inclusive improvement.
- **A new capability lacks a usable entry point.** Technical availability does not produce adoption. Connect the capability to a real journey rather than to a settings page.
- **Trust or comprehension is limiting adoption.** People may hesitate because the system is opaque, surprising, or hard to recover from. Improve the interaction that produces the hesitation.
- **A small prototype would settle an experience question.** A complete build may not be needed to learn what works. Use a representative interaction that exposes the behaviour in question.
- **Different users face competing trades.** A change may help one audience and burden another. Evaluate it across the affected groups rather than for the loudest one.
- **Error states are worse than the happy path.** Failure, recovery, and edge conditions are often unattended and are where trust is actually decided. Design the recovery as deliberately as the success.
- **First use and repeat use need different things.** A design tuned for newcomers may obstruct regulars, or the reverse. Name which population the change serves, and what it costs the other.
- **A workaround has become common.** Users routinely improvising around the product is precise evidence of an unmet need. Design for the behaviour they already demonstrate.
- **Support requests cluster on one interaction.** Repeated questions about the same moment are a design signal, not a documentation gap. Fix the moment rather than explaining it better.

## When not to use
- **No user or system outcome is identified.** A technical capability with no one on the other end is not an experience change.
- **The argument is commercial.** A growth mechanism with no interaction change belongs with the mechanism.
- **The goal is unclear.** Improving an experience towards an undefined outcome optimizes taste.
- **The friction is assumed rather than evidenced.** Redesigning around a guess produces a differently wrong product.
- **A required completion step is what is missing.** Unfinished work is not an experience problem.
- **The question is implementation feasibility.** What can be built is a different claim from what people need.

Route the suggestion elsewhere when one of those signals holds. A commercial mechanism belongs to business and growth, and a feasibility question belongs to technical possibilities. An unfinished obligation belongs to completion, and an undefined outcome belongs to goal clarification. Suspected but unevidenced friction belongs to feedback or investigation, and a change whose effect must be measured belongs to experiment. If the complexity being removed is internal rather than experienced, use simplification.
